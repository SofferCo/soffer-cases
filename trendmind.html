TrendMind is a market-intelligence engine. Every day it scans news, social and filings across thousands of assets, turning the noise into momentum and sentiment signals for stocks and crypto. The technology was real - the product was unreadable.

Its technical founder had built a dense analyst dashboard, but the audience was meant to be everyday investors. I distilled the value and the audience, rebuilt the whole thing as a conversation - and designed and built their website.

Background

signals, not noise

The engine is genuinely strong. It scans premium sources daily - news, social, regulatory filings - across stocks and crypto, and converts them into momentum and sentiment signals.

The original product surfaced all of it in one place: a dark, analyst-grade dashboard with every column a quant could want.

The friction

built for the wrong person

The dashboard wasn't too complex. It was built for the wrong person.

The founder is technical, so the interface spoke his language - dense tables, hype %, sentiment momentum, signal columns. But the audience was never analysts; it was everyday investors. A consumer opens that screen, feels lost, and leaves. The value was real and completely buried.

The insight

Don't simplify the data.
Translate it.

The fix wasn't to strip the engine down - it was to change who does the reading.

Instead of handing people a dashboard, let the engine read it for them and just talk. The product became a front door you speak to, not a spreadsheet you decode.

The solution

meet Esther

Esther - a conversational AI that turns the engine into a chat. Ask "what should I invest in?" or "analyze my portfolio," and it answers like a senior analyst - portfolio-aware, with buy/sell reasoning. Not a generic chatbot.

Under the hood it still scans premium sources every day and burns real compute to do it. What changed is the surface: signals now arrive as a daily brief and a feed anyone can skim.

The result

Same engine.
A product people can actually use.

The dashboard became an app that's live on iOS and Android, holding thousands of conversations a day with 600 paying users.

The technology never changed - what changed was who could read it. The complexity wasn't hidden; it was translated.
